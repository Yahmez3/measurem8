Tablespoons and teaspoons: US, UK and Australian sizes

A teaspoon is 5 mL almost everywhere. A tablespoon is not: Australia's is a third bigger. Here are the sizes, the conversions, and what a spoonful of baking powder, salt, yeast and other staples weighs.

Updated 12 November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

Advertisement

Quick answer: a teaspoon is 5 mL in the US, UK, Australia and New Zealand. A tablespoon is 15 mL (3 teaspoons) in the US, UK and New Zealand, but 20 mL (4 teaspoons) in Australia. A US cup holds 16 tablespoons.

The converter above starts at one teaspoon of baking powder. Change the ingredient for salt, baking soda, yeast or anything else in the list, and switch between US tablespoons, Australian tablespoons and teaspoons.

The American tablespoon is defined as half a US fluid ounce, which is 14.8 mL; NIST rounds it to 15 mL, and US food labels use 15 mL. That 0.2 mL never matters in a kitchen. The Australian tablespoon is the real outlier. As the Australian Women's Weekly puts it, Australia is the only country with a 20 mL tablespoon; the rest use 15 mL. New Zealand uses 15 mL, as Edmonds' cookbook notes. Older British and New Zealand recipes sometimes mention a dessertspoon, usually taken as 10 mL.

When the tablespoon difference matters

An Australian tablespoon holds 5 mL more than the tablespoon in an American, British or New Zealand recipe. For oil, soy sauce or jam, nobody will notice. For anything potent, a third extra is a real mistake: baking soda turns soapy and metallic, salt takes over, yeast over-proofs, and gelatine sets rubbery.

The fix is simple. If you are in Australia following a recipe from elsewhere, measure each tablespoon as 3 level teaspoons. If you are elsewhere following an Australian recipe, measure each tablespoon as 1 tablespoon plus 1 teaspoon. Teaspoons are the same everywhere, so they never need converting.

An Australian cup is 250 mL, which is 12 1/2 Australian tablespoons or 50 teaspoons. For millilitres to spoons: 10 mL is 2 teaspoons, 20 mL is 4 teaspoons (one Australian tablespoon), 50 mL is 3 tablespoons plus 1 teaspoon, and 100 mL is 6 tablespoons plus 2 teaspoons.

A few of these deserve a note. Sources disagree on baking soda: King Arthur weighs half a teaspoon at 3 g, about 6 g a teaspoon, while USDA tables give 4.6 g, so treat it as 5 to 6 g. Cornflour is 8 g a tablespoon by the USDA's cup weight, or about 7 g by King Arthur's. Kosher salt is the big trap: a tablespoon of Morton weighs twice as much as a tablespoon of Diamond Crystal, because Diamond Crystal's crystals are lighter and flakier. If a recipe names one brand and you have the other, convert by weight, or you will double or halve the salt.

Yeast and gelatine

King Arthur Baking weighs 2 1/4 teaspoons of instant yeast at 7 g, and a tablespoon at 9 g. American recipes that call for "one packet" usually mean a 1/4 oz (7 g) packet, so measure about 2 1/4 teaspoons, or weigh 7 g.

Powdered gelatine is the ingredient where the Australian tablespoon bites hardest. Knox says one 7 g envelope, about 2 1/2 teaspoons, sets 2 cups (500 mL) of liquid to a soft gel. Queen, in Australia, says one of its sachets equals 3 teaspoons and sets about 2 cups. Sachet sizes vary by brand, so check the packet, and measure gelatine in teaspoons, never in Australian tablespoons, when following a recipe from elsewhere.

Measure spoons properly

Use measuring spoons, not cutlery: everyday spoons vary widely in size. For dry ingredients, scoop and then sweep the top level with the back of a knife; a heaped spoon holds much more than a level one. Recipes that say "rounded" mean a gentle mound, and "heaped" means as much as the spoon will carry. For liquids, fill to the brim over a small bowl, not over the mixing bowl.
