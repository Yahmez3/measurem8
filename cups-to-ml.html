Cups to mL for liquids

How many millilitres are in a cup depends on whose cup it is. Charts for US, Australian and UK cups, the carton sizes you actually buy, and what a cup of water, milk or stock weighs.

Updated 12 November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

Advertisement

Quick answer: a cup in a modern American recipe is 240 mL. An American measuring cup from the shops holds 236.6 mL. An Australian or New Zealand cup is 250 mL. The old British cup, still found in some vintage cookbooks, is 284 mL.

The converter above starts on the US cup. Switch the "From" box to Cup (Australian metric) if your recipe is Australian, or use the charts below, which cover every fraction you are likely to meet. For liquids such as water, stock and milk, millilitres are also a stand-in for grams, which makes a kitchen scale the easiest measuring jug you own. More on that further down.

Why there is more than one cup

The cup started life as a loose household measure and was tidied up differently in each country. The United States defines its customary cup as half a US pint, which works out to exactly 236.5882365 mL. American food labels, though, use a rounded "legal" cup of 240 mL, and most recipe developers and published density charts now write to that figure. Australia and New Zealand went metric in the 1970s and fixed the cup at a clean 250 mL. Britain mostly abandoned cups for weights, but older British books use a cup of half an imperial pint, about 284 mL.

For a splash of milk in a sauce, none of this matters. It starts to matter in baking and in anything you scale up. The gap between a US recipe cup and an Australian cup is 10 mL, about 4%. Over four cups of liquid that is 40 mL, roughly two and a half tablespoons, which is enough to loosen a batter or thin a custard. Follow an old British recipe with an Australian cup and you are short instead: each British cup is 34 mL more than yours.

Two handy landmarks fall out of the table. Four US measuring cups make a US quart, 946 mL, which is why a 32 fl oz carton of American stock is "4 cups". Four Australian cups make exactly one litre.

Which cup does my recipe use?

Most recipes never say, but they leave clues. Fahrenheit oven temperatures, sticks of butter and ounces by weight point to an American recipe, so assume 240 mL. Celsius temperatures with cups, especially alongside 20 mL tablespoons, point to Australia or New Zealand, so assume 250 mL. Gas marks, imperial pints and ounces point to an older British recipe; if it uses cups at all, they are probably the 284 mL kind. Our guide to US, UK and Australian measurements covers the spoon and pint differences, which are bigger than the cup ones.

What a cup of liquid weighs

Water is the one liquid where volume and weight line up perfectly: one millilitre weighs one gram, so a US recipe cup of water weighs 240 g and an Australian cup weighs 250 g. Stock and broth are mostly water and weigh almost the same. Milk is a touch heavier because of its dissolved sugars and proteins, while oil is lighter and floats for the same reason. Syrups are the heavyweights.

Worked example: 365 grams of water in cups

Because a gram of water is a millilitre, 365 g of water is 365 mL. Divide by 240 and you get 1.52 US recipe cups, which you can measure as 1 1/2 cups plus a teaspoon. In Australian cups it is 365 ÷ 250, or 1.46 cups. The same shortcut works for stock: a recipe asking for 500 g of stock wants 500 mL, a little over two US cups.

Worked example: one cup of vegetable broth in mL

One cup of broth in an American recipe is 240 mL. If you are pouring from an Australian measuring cup, fill it to just under the top, since your cup holds 250 mL. A standard 1 litre carton holds a little over four US cups, so a recipe that wants four cups of broth will leave you about 40 mL, roughly two and a half tablespoons, spare.

How to measure liquids accurately

Use a clear jug with printed markings, not the nested cups meant for flour and sugar. Dry cups have to be filled to the brim, and nobody walks a brim-full cup of milk across the kitchen without losing some. Put the jug on the bench, pour, then bend down so your eye is level with the line. Looking from above makes the liquid seem higher than it is, and you end up short.

For sticky liquids like honey and golden syrup, a quick wipe of oil inside the cup stops a few grams clinging to the sides, or skip the cup entirely and weigh them using the table above. For anything water-based over a cup or two, weighing is simply faster: set the bowl on the scale, zero it, and pour until the number matches the millilitres you need.

Spoons in a cup

A US cup holds 16 tablespoons or 48 teaspoons. An Australian cup holds 12 1/2 Australian tablespoons, because the Australian tablespoon is 20 mL rather than 15 mL, or 50 teaspoons. That spoon difference is the one that wrecks recipes, particularly with baking soda and gelatine, and the measurements guide explains how to sidestep it. Sources

  • US customary cup: NIST Handbook 44, Appendix C, defines the cup as exactly 8 US fluid ounces; with the US fluid ounce at 29.5735295625 mL (1/128 of the 3.785411784 L US gallon), that is 236.5882365 mL.
  • US 240 mL cup: 21 CFR 101.9(b)(5)(viii), the FDA nutrition-labelling rule: "a cup means 240 mL".
  • UK imperial pint and fluid ounce: Weights and Measures Act 1985, Schedule 1 (gallon 4.54609 L; pint one-eighth of a gallon, 568.26125 mL). The old British cup is half that pint.
  • Australian and New Zealand measures (250 mL cup, 20 mL tablespoon, 5 mL teaspoon) are the metric kitchen measures adopted during metrication in the 1970s and printed in Australian and New Zealand cookbooks today.
  • Liquid weights: USDA FoodData Central (SR Legacy: canned beef broth, 1 cup = 240 g; whole milk, 1 cup = 244 g per US measuring cup) and the King Arthur Baking ingredient weight chart, scaled to the cup sizes shown.
