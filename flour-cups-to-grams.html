Flour cups to grams

Flour is the hardest ingredient to measure by the cup. The same cup can hold 120 g or 160 g depending on how you fill it. Here is what each kind of flour weighs, and how to get the number your recipe expects.

Updated 12 November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

Advertisement

Quick answer: one cup of plain or all-purpose flour weighs about 120 g when you spoon it into the cup and level it, the figure King Arthur Baking uses. USDA reference tables give 125 g. Scoop the cup straight through the bag and it can hold up to 160 g. Bread and cake flour weigh about the same as plain; wholemeal and self-raising flour about 113 g.

The converter above uses the spooned figure, 120 g per US recipe cup. Change the ingredient for bread, cake, wholemeal, self-raising, pastry, rye or almond flour, or switch the units to Australian cups and tablespoons.

Why flour gives different answers

Flour is a fine powder full of air, and it compresses easily. It settles in the bag on the way home, packs down under its own weight in the canister, and compacts further when you push a measuring cup through it. King Arthur Baking's test kitchen puts a carefully filled cup of all-purpose flour at around 120 g and says a compacted cup can hold up to 160 g. That is a third more flour from the same cup, enough to turn a tender cake dry and a soft cookie into a biscuit.

Most published cup weights, and the recipes developed alongside them, assume the lighter, spooned measure. If your bakes from American recipes keep coming out heavy or dry, the way you fill the cup is the first thing to check.

How to measure a cup of flour

King Arthur's method has three steps. First, fluff the flour in its bag or canister with a spoon to loosen it. Second, spoon it into the measuring cup until it overflows, without pressing it down or tapping the cup. Third, sweep the back of a knife across the rim to level it. Done that way, a cup comes out close to 120 g every time.

Watch the wording on sifted flour, too. "1 cup flour, sifted" means measure the flour first and sift it afterwards, so it weighs a normal cup. "1 cup sifted flour" means sift first and then spoon the sifted flour into the cup, which gives a lighter cup because sifting adds air. If a recipe gives grams as well as cups, use the grams and the problem disappears.

Why some Australian charts say 150 g a cup

If you have used an Australian conversion chart, you may have seen a much bigger number. The Australian Women's Weekly chart lists plain and self-raising flour at 150 g per 250 mL cup, and wholemeal at 160 g. Other Australian charts disagree: New Idea's lists flour at 125 g a cup, which is what a spooned cup gives. Neither chart explains its method, but the gap is most likely measuring style rather than a different flour: 150 g is what a firmer, scooped cup gives, well inside the range King Arthur describes for compacted flour.

The practical rule: if an Australian recipe gives grams, weigh them and ignore the cups. If it gives cups only and comes from a book that publishes the 150 g conversion, its author probably measured that way, so scoop rather than spoon. For American recipes, spoon and level, or use 120 g a cup.

Flour names around the world

American all-purpose flour is the close equivalent of plain flour in Australia and the UK. American bread flour corresponds to the flour sold in Britain as strong white flour, and wholemeal is the British and Australian name for whole wheat. Self-raising (self-rising in the US) is flour with raising agent already mixed in; formulas differ between brands and countries, so if a recipe is fussy about rise, use plain flour plus the baking powder the recipe specifies.

What separates the flours is mostly protein, which decides how much gluten a dough can build. King Arthur gives its own flours as about 11.7% protein for all-purpose, 12.7% for bread flour, 10% for cake flour, 8% for pastry flour and 13.2% for whole wheat. Other brands vary, and plain flour in other countries is not always identical to American all-purpose, but the order holds: cake and pastry flour for tender crumbs, bread flour for chew.

Weighing is the cure for all of this. A cheap digital scale makes flour the easiest ingredient in the kitchen instead of the hardest. For everything else you bake with, see the cups to grams chart, the printable kitchen chart and the baking conversion guide.
