Evaporated and condensed milk conversions

Two thick, canned milks that look alike, weigh differently and behave nothing alike in a recipe. Cups to grams for both, what each can size holds in cups, and when one can stand in for the other.

Updated 12 November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

Advertisement

Quick answer: one cup (240 mL) of evaporated milk weighs about 256 g. One cup of sweetened condensed milk weighs about 310 g. In a 236.6 mL American measuring cup the USDA figures are 252 g and 306 g.

The converter above is set to evaporated milk. Change the ingredient to Sweetened condensed milk for the other one, or switch the units to Australian cups and tablespoons. The charts below cover the measures recipes actually use.

Evaporated milk and condensed milk are not the same thing

Both start as ordinary milk with much of the water cooked off, which is why both are thicker and heavier than fresh milk. Nestlé, which makes Carnation, describes evaporated milk as fresh milk with about 60% of its water removed. Nothing else is added, so it is unsweetened, a little caramel-tasting from the heating, and runny enough to pour like cream.

Sweetened condensed milk is concentrated in the same way, then sweetened heavily with sugar. The sugar is what makes it so thick it barely moves when you tip the can, and it is also why a cup of it weighs about a fifth more than a cup of evaporated milk. It belongs in fudge, caramel slices, key lime pie and Vietnamese coffee, where that sweetness is the point.

The two cans sit side by side on the shelf, often with near-identical branding. Grabbing the wrong one is the most common mistake, and it is not fixable afterwards, so check the label for the word "sweetened" before you open it.

How many cups are in a can?

This is the question recipes create when they say "one can" and you are holding a different country's can, or when they say "1 1/2 cups" and you want to know whether one can is enough. Evaporated milk is sold by volume in the US and Australia but by weight in the UK; condensed milk is sold by weight everywhere. The table converts each common size both ways.

The useful takeaway: the main evaporated milk cans in all three countries hold roughly a cup and a half, and the condensed milk cans hold about a cup and a quarter. An American recipe that wants "one 14-ounce can" of condensed milk works with an Australian 395 g can or a British 397 g tin without adjustment. Can sizes do change, and store brands vary, so the label on the can in your hand always wins.

Can you swap them?

Evaporated milk for fresh milk

Yes, once you add the water back. Nestlé's own guidance for Carnation is one part evaporated milk to one and a quarter parts water. Many American recipes simply say equal parts, which gives a slightly richer milk; for baking and sauces either works. The flavour is faintly cooked rather than fresh, which nobody will notice in a cake but you might in a glass of milk or a delicate custard.

Fresh milk for evaporated milk

In soups, mashed potato and savoury sauces, plain milk works with a little less richness. In recipes that depend on evaporated milk's low water content, such as pumpkin pie, fudge or a set custard, swapping straight in will leave the result runny. Simmer the fresh milk gently, stirring, until it has reduced by about half, cool it, and measure out what the recipe needs.

Condensed milk and evaporated milk for each other

No, not directly. Nestlé says plainly that the two should not be used as substitutes for one another. Condensed milk would add a large dose of sugar to a recipe that never expected it, and evaporated milk would leave a sweet recipe flat and unset. If a recipe calls for one and you only have the other, it is usually better to pick a different recipe.

Measuring tips

Evaporated milk pours easily and measures like any liquid: use a clear jug and read it at eye level, as described in our cups to mL guide. Condensed milk is a different story. It clings to cups and spoons, so a measured cup can lose several grams to the sides. Either wipe the cup with a little oil first, or skip the cup and weigh it straight into the bowl using the chart above. To get every bit out of the can, scrape it with a flexible spatula, and if the recipe calls for the whole can, simply use the whole can rather than measuring at all.
