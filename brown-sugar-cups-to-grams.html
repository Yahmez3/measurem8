Brown sugar cups to grams

Brown sugar is the one dry ingredient recipes want pressed into the cup. Packed or loose changes the weight by a third, so here is what each measure really holds.

Updated 12 November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

Advertisement

Quick answer: one cup of firmly packed brown sugar weighs about 220 g, the figure used by the USDA and by the Australian Women's Weekly. King Arthur Baking uses a slightly lighter 213 g. Spooned in loosely, the same cup holds only about 145 g. Light and dark brown sugar weigh the same.

The converter above uses the packed figure, 220 g a cup, since that is what American and Australian recipes mean by "a cup of brown sugar". Switch the units to Australian cups or tablespoons if you need them, and use the charts below for the usual fractions.

Why brown sugar gets packed

Brown sugar is white sugar crystals coated in molasses, and that sticky film makes it clump. Spoon it loosely into a cup and the clumps leave pockets of air, so two people measuring "a cup" can end up with very different amounts. Pressing it down squeezes the air out and gives a repeatable measure. That is why recipes say "firmly packed", and why USDA figures put a packed cup at 220 g against about 145 g for an unpacked one. Measure loosely when a recipe expects packed and you have used about a third less sugar than the author did, enough to make cookies spread less, brown less and taste noticeably plainer.

To pack a cup, spoon the sugar in and press it down firmly with the back of the spoon or your fingers, then add more and press again until it is level with the rim. When you tip it into the bowl it should hold the shape of the cup, like a sandcastle. If it collapses into a heap, it was not packed.

Light, dark and the rest

Light and dark brown sugar differ in how much molasses they carry, not in how much a cup weighs; King Arthur Baking gives one weight for both. Indiana Sugars, a US supplier, puts light brown sugar at about 3.5% molasses and dark at about 6.5%, though the exact amount varies by brand. Dark brown sugar tastes deeper and more toffee-like, and swapping one for the other by the cup changes the flavour, not the quantities.

In Australia, brown sugar is sold as plain "brown sugar", CSR's in 500 g and 1 kg packs, and Australian recipes ask for it firmly packed just as American ones do. In the UK the names differ: "soft light brown sugar" is the closest match to American light brown, and light or dark muscovado is a less refined, stickier sugar with a stronger molasses flavour. UK packs are usually 500 g, and UK recipes almost always give brown sugar in grams, so the cup question rarely comes up. Demerara is a different product altogether, with large dry crystals; measure it like granulated sugar and never pack it.

Out of brown sugar?

A common kitchen fix is to mix white sugar with molasses: about 1 tablespoon of molasses per cup of granulated sugar for light brown, and 2 tablespoons for dark. Rub it together with your fingertips until the colour is even. Indiana Sugars suggests the same idea in the other direction, adding about a tablespoon of molasses to a cup of light brown sugar to approximate dark. In the UK, black treacle is the usual stand-in for molasses.

If your brown sugar has gone hard

Brown sugar sets rock-hard when its moisture escapes. King Arthur Baking's suggestions are to seal it in a container with a slice of bread overnight, to use a terracotta brown sugar saver, or, for an immediate fix, to work in about 3/4 teaspoon of water per 8 oz (227 g) of sugar. Storing it airtight from the start prevents the problem.
