How to print it

Press the button above, or use your browser's print command. The page prints as a single sheet on A4 or US Letter paper: the site's header, footer and this explanation drop away, leaving just the chart. If your printer squeezes it onto two pages, choose "Fit to page" or a scale of about 90% in the print dialog. It reads fine in black and white.

Where the numbers come from

Every figure on the chart is the same one the MeasureMate converter uses, so the chart and the tool always agree. Cup weights assume a US recipe cup of 240 mL with dry ingredients spooned into the cup and levelled, the convention used by modern American recipes and by published weight charts such as King Arthur Baking's. Brown sugar is the exception and is firmly packed, as recipes expect. Butter is based on the US stick of exactly four ounces.

The ingredient pages show the working and the sources: cups to grams for the full ingredient list, butter, brown sugar, evaporated and condensed milk and cups to mL for liquids.

If your cups are Australian

An Australian cup holds 250 mL, about 4% more than the US recipe cup the chart uses. For one cup of anything that is a few grams, well inside normal kitchen variation. For recipes that use several cups, or for baking where ratios matter, add roughly 4% to the cup weights, or better, weigh the ingredients. The tablespoon difference matters more: an Australian tablespoon is 20 mL, a third bigger than the US and UK 15 mL tablespoon. For baking soda, baking powder, salt and gelatine in a foreign recipe, measure three level teaspoons instead of one Australian tablespoon. The US, UK and Australian measurements guide covers the rest.

Oven temperatures and fan ovens

The temperatures are the rounded pairs recipe writers actually use, not exact conversions: 180 °C is really 356 °F, but every recipe says 350. Fan-forced ovens move hot air around the food, so they cook like a conventional oven set about 20 °C higher. Most recipes give the conventional temperature, so if your oven has a fan, use the fan-forced column. The baking conversion guide explains when each temperature is used.

When to skip the chart and weigh

Cups are convenient, but even careful cooks get different weights from the same cup, especially with flour. For bread, pastry, macarons and anything where the recipe gives grams, a digital scale is faster and more accurate than any chart. Keep the chart for everything else: the quick weeknight bake, the recipe card from a relative, and the American cookbook that only speaks in cups and sticks.
