Butter conversions: sticks, cups, tablespoons and grams

American recipes measure butter in sticks and cups. Most of the world buys it in 250 g blocks. Here is how the two line up, measure by measure.

Updated 12 November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

Advertisement

Quick answer: one US stick of butter is 1/2 cup, 8 tablespoons, 4 ounces or 113 g. One cup is two sticks, 227 g. One tablespoon is about 14 g. A 250 g block, the standard size in Australia, New Zealand and the UK, is 2.2 sticks, a little over one cup.

The converter above starts at one stick. Change the amount for more, or switch the "From" box to cups, tablespoons or Australian tablespoons. The charts below cover the measures recipes actually use, in both directions.

What a stick of butter is

In the US, butter is usually sold in one-pound boxes of four sticks, each wrapped separately. Land O'Lakes and Cabot define a stick the same way: half a cup, eight tablespoons, four ounces, or 113 g. The wrapper is printed with tablespoon lines so you can cut off what you need. American shelves carry two shapes, long slim sticks in the east and short stubby ones in the west, but both hold the same four ounces. That makes "a stick of butter" one of the more reliable measures in American cooking, because it is really a weight in disguise.

Using a 250 g block for an American recipe

Outside the US, butter comes in blocks. In Australia, Western Star sells its butter in 250 g and 500 g blocks; in the UK, Anchor, Kerrygold and Lurpak all come in 250 g blocks. Recipes from those countries give butter in grams, often as "125 g butter, softened", which is simply half a block. In the US, imported Kerrygold is sold as an 8 oz foil block, two sticks' worth.

An American recipe that wants "1 cup (2 sticks) butter" needs 227 g. Cut a 250 g block at the point where 23 g, a little under a tenth of the block, is left over, and keep that end for greasing the tin. For half a cup, cut the block in half and shave a thin slice off one half: 125 g against the 113 g wanted. Most butter cakes and cookies will not notice the extra 12 g; shortbread, pastry and anything with a precise butter-to-flour ratio will, so trim it.

Recipes often say "1/2 cup butter, melted". That means measure the butter solid, then melt it: one stick, 113 g. Melting does not change the weight, so if you are working in grams the number stays the same.

Salted or unsalted?

Most baking recipes are written for unsalted butter so the cook controls the salt. Salted butter is fine in its place; you just account for the salt it carries. USDA FoodData Central lists salted butter at 643 mg of sodium per 100 g in its long-standing reference entry and 524 mg in a newer analysed sample. Converted to table salt, that is roughly 1/4 to 1/3 teaspoon in every stick, or a little over 1/2 teaspoon in a 250 g block.

So if a recipe wants unsalted butter and you only have salted, cut the recipe's added salt by about 1/4 teaspoon per stick, or 1/2 teaspoon per 250 g block. If the recipe has less added salt than that to remove, leave it out and accept a slightly saltier bake. Going the other way, a recipe written for salted butter will taste a little flat with unsalted; add a pinch of salt per stick.

Not all butter is the same butter

Butter is mostly milk fat, with water and a little milk solids making up the rest, and the law sets a floor. In the US, butter must contain at least 80% milk fat by weight. The Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code sets the same 80% minimum, and so do the EU rules that the UK carried over. Some premium and "European-style" butters sit above that minimum, which means a little less water in each block. In a cake you will not taste the difference, but bakers often reach for higher-fat butter for pastry and shortbread, so it is worth noticing when a recipe asks for it.

How to measure butter without a scale

The wrapper is your best tool. American sticks are marked in tablespoons along the paper, so slice straight through the wrapper at the line you need. Without markings, cut a stick in half for 1/4 cup and in quarters for 2 tablespoons; each stick is close enough to uniform for that to work.

For an awkward amount from a block, use water displacement. Pour cold water into a clear measuring jug, say to 250 mL, then push pieces of butter under the surface until the level rises by the volume you need. Butter floats, so hold it down with a spoon handle while you read the line. Pour off the water and you have your butter. It is a bit fiddly, but it is far more accurate than packing soft butter into a measuring cup, which traps air and leaves the cup coated.
