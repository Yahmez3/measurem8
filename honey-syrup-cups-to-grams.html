Honey and syrup cups to grams

Honey and syrups are heavy for their size and cling to everything. A cup weighs far more than a cup of water, and the easiest way to measure them is not with a cup at all.

Updated 12 November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

Advertisement

Quick answer: one tablespoon of honey weighs about 21 g and one cup about 340 g (USDA: 339 g). A cup of maple syrup is about 315 g, golden syrup about 336 g (350 g in an Australian cup), and molasses about 340 g.

The converter above starts at one tablespoon of honey. Change the ingredient for maple syrup, golden syrup, molasses, agave or corn syrup, or switch the units to Australian tablespoons and cups, which are bigger.

Why a cup of honey weighs so much

Honey is mostly sugar dissolved in a small amount of water, and sugar is denser than water. Its specific gravity is around 1.43, so a cup weighs about 1.4 times as much as a cup of water. The exact figure shifts with water content: the UN Food and Agriculture Organization's honey reference shows specific gravity falling from about 1.45 for honey with 13% water to about 1.40 at 21%. That is why different charts give honey anywhere from 336 g to 340 g per US cup, and why honey with more water in it is slightly lighter by the cup than drier honey.

The other syrups follow the same rule. The more concentrated the sugar, the heavier the cup. Maple syrup is a little thinner than honey and lighter by the cup; molasses and golden syrup are close to honey.

Sources disagree by a few percent, and that is normal for natural products. For honey, the USDA gives 339 g a cup and King Arthur Baking 21 g a tablespoon; the Australian Women's Weekly chart rounds an Australian cup of honey up to 360 g. Corn syrup varies the most: King Arthur lists 312 g a cup, while USDA-based tables run higher, around 341 g. None of these differences will show in a recipe.

A tin of golden syrup is a little under a cup and a half, so an Anzac biscuit recipe that wants two tablespoons will barely dent it, while a recipe that wants a full cup and a half will empty a 454 g tin and need a little from a second.

How to measure honey and syrup

The cleanest method is to skip the cup. Put the mixing bowl or saucepan on a digital scale, zero it, and pour until you reach the weight from the table. Nothing sticks to a cup you never used, and you get the full amount instead of leaving a film behind.

If you must use spoons or cups, coat them first with a thin film of oil or a quick spray, and the syrup will slide out. When a recipe also calls for oil or melted butter, measure that first in the same cup and the syrup will follow it out cleanly. Warm syrups pour more easily too: stand a jar of thick or set honey in a bowl of warm water for a few minutes before measuring. Level spoons by eye rather than letting syrup pile up, because a heaped tablespoon holds noticeably more than the level one the recipe means.

Swapping one syrup for another

Australian and British cooks often reach for golden syrup where an American recipe uses light corn syrup, and the two behave similarly in sticky toppings and pecan pie, though golden syrup brings a buttery, caramel flavour. Honey, maple syrup, golden syrup and agave can usually stand in for each other by volume in glazes, dressings and flapjacks, with a change in flavour rather than texture. Molasses is the exception: its strong, slightly bitter taste takes over, so swap it only where the recipe can carry it. Replacing granulated sugar with any syrup is a bigger change, because syrups add liquid and brown faster; it is safer to follow a recipe written for the syrup you have.
